# What the field MOCKS in vertical-video captions, 2026 — fetched research

**Provenance / how much to trust this.** Gathered 2026-08-23 by a background
research agent under a NON-INTROSPECTIVE brief (no answering from model
knowledge; every finding must carry a fetched source). Method was `yt-dlp`
search + `--write-comments` (top-sorted, replies included) across ~40 videos;
every quote was read out of a JSON the agent pulled.

**What this evidence IS:** YouTube commenters, with like-counts as a crude
weight. Loud, real, and dated — but an audience venting, not a controlled study.
A 7,100-like comment tells you a sentiment is widely shared; it does not tell you
it costs views.

**What this evidence is NOT:** measurement. Nothing here says caption treatment
moved a view count. That territory is still unresearched — see the gap at the
bottom.

**Claude has not independently re-verified any individual link.**

Reproduced verbatim from the agent's report — not re-worded, not summarised.

## 2. Arguments visibly happening

### Word-by-word vs. phrase chunks — the LOUDEST split, and it is live

**Against (readability/accessibility):**

> "It makes them unreasonably hard to read, you have to focus completely on the subtitles to actually see them. With regular subtitles you can read ahead, focus back on the video, and only read another when the next sentence starts. Just glancing down now and then. **But if you look away from individual word subtitles you'll miss that word and have no idea what it was.** It's stupid."
> — @doltBmB, 11 likes, Xslo9OK3OCE

> "These single-word captions… **don't work as proper subtitles/closed captions since they pop up too quickly to keep up with (so they wouldn't even help people that need them).**" — @XaneMyers, 350 likes, Xslo9OK3OCE

> "It also removes the original purpose for subtitles; deaf people. Usually, hard of hearing people need long pieces of text so they can flip between video and subtitles, but now if they look away from the subtitles for one second they can lose vital context." — @drredherring, Xslo9OK3OCE

> "I have to put my thumb over the word to be able to even pay attention to the video itself" — @Asukol, 51 likes, Xslo9OK3OCE

> "subtitles are really useful... BUT NOT WHEN YOU SPAM THEM IN A WAY YOU CAN'T READ" — @Miaou333, Xslo9OK3OCE

> "makes me really wish those subtitles were aggressively regulated under closed captioning and accessibility" — @Diddz, 20 likes, Xslo9OK3OCE

> "having them be read by every single word instead of laying out a full sentence like how they're supposed to be" — @outphase78, 45 likes, Xslo9OK3OCE

An independent, older instance of the same complaint about auto-caption behaviour, from a 17.4M-view video:

> "I really hate reading auto generated subs, because **they only ever appear one word at a time which makes it really hard to grasp the structure of the sentence** or focus on anything beside the subs."
> — @Akky7032, **1,800 likes**, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VYJtb2YXae8 (Vox, "Why we all need subtitles now", 2023-01-20)

**For (mechanism, not aesthetics) — the one substantive defence found:**

> "A long, long time ago (like 2005-2010) I remember an online 'speed reading' website demo which showed that you could read much faster if each word was highlighted in sequence, rather than a solid block of text or scrolling ticker. If I had to guess, I'd say that the reason the 'one word at a time' style of subtitles has greater retention, because the rapid on screen motion triggers some kind dopamine, like a baby's mobile but for adults, and also because it takes less brainpower to read subtitles in that format, so it's the attention equivalent of tictacs"
> — @Plluw, **96 likes**, Xslo9OK3OCE

**The middle position — rolling window beats single-word pop:**

> "I feel this particular style of subtitles/text is more engaging and is much more easy to follow **instead of the fast, one word popping in and out subtitles.**"
> — @foob2066, 2 likes, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LIOZpc5LgzY ("Word by Word Text Animation In DaVinci Resolve", 2024-07-25, 455k views)

**Professional framing of the same split** — Premiere 26.3 shipped a Single-Word Captions feature and an editor's video description argues against it as a default (video description, verbatim):

> "Single-word captions in Premiere 26.3 are everywhere right now — **but they're a style choice, not a readability upgrade.** … **Why it's a rhythm effect, not an accessibility fix** … When single-word captions help your edit — and when they hurt retention … The workaround for the karaoke-style highlight look … **Fast cuts vs. tutorial pacing: which caption style fits which**"
> — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Aw9KXoAI9rA ("Single-Word Captions in Premiere 26.3: When to Use Them (and When Not To)", 2026-07-16)

Its one substantive comment:

> "using single-word captions that are overused or improperly used **can break accessibility rules. It's because the single-word captions break logical flow.** … This feature is strongly discouraged for readability and can be strictly forbidden for accessibility. **Fixing single-word captions by merging one-word-at-a-time captions can help balancing readability and engagement.** (Edit: If the caption needs to be readable, the caption must follow logical flow.)"
> — @ItsNakhwanMaulana, same URL

## 3. Serious/documentary vs. hustle/entertainment treatment

**No comment thread** was found where someone argues explicitly "serious content needs different captions than hustle content." What was found is the split expressed through **what professionals label as separate products**, and through what the documentary-taste audience praises:

- A dedicated tutorial exists for the opposite register, titled and described in opposition to the viral style — "How to create **clean and smooth** documentary style captions… **clean & minimal** text animations… cinematic and professional look" — https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h35eqlQLKCg (2026-04-09). Same creator sells "Viral Caption Animation Pack" separately, i.e. the two are marketed as different things.
- The Vox/editorial audience explicitly praises the *absence* of the shorts-caption toolkit:
> "13:20 it's absolute fantastic! just 3 color - and picture look SO BEAUTIFULL! **No drop shadow, no deep glow, just text and dynamic blur with noise** 🤯"
> — @taylls5, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CqHb6bVoxgI ("How VOX Directs Your Attention", 2026-04-29)

- Film-subtitling craft convention, from the StudioBinder subtitling guide's comments — **text is moved to avoid faces, and never lands on a cut**:
> "In Japan, for instance, you're expected to **move the text to the left or right to avoid key elements in the frame, particularly characters' faces.** Also **text disappears two frames before a cut and appears two frames after a cut**, rather than on the cut like I would do it here in the US… the old, white 'teletype' font with no outline or shadow"
> — @CantankerousDave, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5Y3Em-IMSwM ("Subtitles — The Ultimate Guide to Movie Subtitling Format, Style & Etiquette", 2023-07-31, 197k views)

- Same thread, on **all-caps as a deliberate tonal choice, not a default**:
> "One of my favourite stylistic subtitles is in Blade Runner 2049. **The text is all caps and shaded in a murky grey, emphasising the gritty nature of the setting.**" — @abcloete, same URL

- And on punctuation as tone-carrier — directly applicable to a judge/defendant exchange:
> "I have watched plenty of subtitled movies where the character is screaming or excited, and the subtitles have periods instead of exclamation points… **Subtitles DON'T enhance the film if the punctuation is off.**" — @noman6041, 4 likes, same URL

- Reading-rate discipline is a stated professional constraint: "we have small character limits to keep under for only a short amount of time" (@starrystudy00, 25 likes, same URL); the video's own chapter list includes "Chapter 2: Rules of Subtitling" at 04:49 (@StudioBinder pinned, 38 likes).

## 4. Credible vs. clickbait in caption treatment

Assembled from the above, all sourced:

**Reads as clickbait/slop** — burned-in word-by-word captions (@TheNathanNS 7.1k, @NefashusLP 2k); white-on-yellow highlight (@MichGamerXD 51); yellow/white text as an AI colour scheme (@mako1717); colour-cycling per word (@spaceyberry); a different font per word (@keskinen2); captions visible in the thumbnail — an explicit no-click trigger (@XaneMyers 350; @mossmyth1123); emoji in the caption stack (@NefashusLP 2k); ping SFX on each caption pop (@NefashusLP 2k, @dumbbirdwayne 340); anything that "appears to be automatic" (@davidsbalencar 21).

**Reads as credible** — phrase-length lines you can read ahead of (@doltBmB, @Akky7032 1.8k); accurate transcription, because inaccuracy is what commenters describe as brain-poisoning (@GeorgeTsiros, @snippingtool7810 45); minimal treatment, no glow/no drop shadow (@taylls5); text kept off faces and off cuts (@CantankerousDave); caps and punctuation used as deliberate tone signals rather than defaults (@abcloete, @noman6041).

## THE GAP THAT REMAINS

Everything above is **sentiment**. Nobody here measured a view count.

The unrun territory: **courtroom-shorts winners vs losers, measured** — top vs
bottom performing videos on the SAME channel, caption treatment measured in
pixels off extracted frames. That agent was stopped before reporting. Until it
runs, we know what a vocal audience says it hates; we do not know what costs
