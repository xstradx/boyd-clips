# 2. The Montserrat/Anton/Impact + white-with-yellow-highlighted-active-word rolling window — specifically

**What it is, verbatim, from the vendor/practitioner-consensus side** (this is marketing copy, label it as such, but it pins the exact parameters):
> "The best-performing TikTok caption style in 2026 is word-by-word or short-phrase captions with high-contrast colors (white text with black outline, or yellow with black outline), positioned in the lower-middle third of the frame."
> "Style 1: The Bold Highlight — White text, all caps or title case, with a key word or phrase color-highlighted in yellow, red, or orange."
> "Bold sans-serif fonts dominate TikTok captions — similar to Montserrat Bold, Proxima Nova Bold, or Impact."
— "TikTok Caption Styles 2026", Blitzcut, https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-style-tiktok

So: yes, it is a **shipped template**, not a design decision. It ships in CapCut, Captions.ai and Submagic presets — the same page lists "CapCut (multiple caption styles include word highlighting), Captions.ai (word-by-word with highlight options)."

**Is it treated as a tell?** Three separate readings, and they do not agree:

**(a) A tell of a beginner / a bought-cheap edit** — see §1. Strongest evidence.

**(b) A tell of inaccessibility, from the deaf accessibility community.** This is the most substantive body of criticism and it is aimed *precisely* at the multi-colour rolling highlight:
> "Karaoke-style captions are not accessible as it doesn't allow the viewer to read at their own pace."
— Meryl Evans, CPACC (deaf; accessibility consultant), 6 Jan 2025, https://x.com/merylkevans/status/1876314305903436013

> "This is why we have captioning best practices. You'll never make 100% of the viewers happy. Instead, you can make the majority happy when you follow best practices. And that's not Hormozi / dynamic captions."
— Meryl Evans, 2 Jan 2025, https://x.com/merylkevans/status/1874857186087539102

> "There's a reason captions are boring and have been boring all these years. Because they work."
> "They are not meant to be the star. The video is supposed to be the star."
> "This Hormozi stuff is taking away from that."
— Meryl Evans, quoted by Jeff Frick, "Boring Captions Work Better", *Turn the Lens*, 29 Mar 2026, https://turnthelens.substack.com/p/boring-captions-work-better

Frick's own framing in the same post:
> "I'm talking about Hormozi captions. This desire to add theater-like animation with pops and sizzles and changes in color, shape, and size, and think that that is a value, when really it's a distraction from the point, the star, the video."
> "Spend the investment on accuracy not animation"

And the specific attack on the **two-colour** mechanic:
> "I've seen videos with more than one color in the caption text. The recommendation is to use one color for multiple reasons. For one, many people are colorblind or color-sensitive."
> "People will notice the captions when it has more than one color. You want viewers to grasp what the captions say, not what they look like."
> "Subtitles are meant to be plain and readable. They're not for branding or getting creative."
> "The key to great captions is invisibility. … Great captions are boring!"
— Meryl Evans, "10 Rules You Need to Create Great Captioned Videos", meryl.net, published 7 Apr 2020, last modified 10 Oct 2024, https://meryl.net/10-rules-you-need-to-create-great-captioned-videos/

**(c) Not a tell at all — it's the pro move.** The opposing side, kept visible:
> "Version A (beginner): - Slow start - Static captions - No B-roll - Weak audio / Version B (pro): - Instant hook - Animated captions - Dynamic B-roll - Clean sound + intentional effects / The difference in retention is usually 30-50%."
— Hasnain Deswali (@H_deswali), 18 Aug 2026, https://x.com/H_deswali/status/2089833715514188007
*(No data behind the 30–50% figure; the poster sells editing services. Weigh accordingly — but it is a real, live position.)*

And a genuine counter from *inside* the accessibility camp, which is why this is a split and not a consensus:
> "Some may benefit, sometimes people who have ADHD benefit from having the motion so they can focus better because they need to have two things going on."
— Meryl Evans, in "I find the waves distracting", *Turn the Lens*, 3 Dec 2024, https://turnthelens.substack.com/p/i-find-the-waves-distracting

# 3. The live arguments

### ALL CAPS vs sentence case — **written standards are unanimously against all-caps; it is not a taste question**

- **BBC** (Subtitle Guidelines, v1 Sept 2016, updated continuously; hosted at https://www.bbc.co.uk/accessibility/forproducts/guides/subtitles/):
  > §11.2 Stress: "Use caps to indicate when a word is stressed. Do not overuse this device - text sprinkled with caps can be hard to read." … "Use caps also to indicate when words are shouted or screamed: HELP ME! However, avoid large chunks of text in caps as they can be hard to read."
  > §16.2: "…as these labels are often fairly long and a large amount of text in upper case is hard to read."
- **DCMP Captioning Key** (US Dept. of Education-funded, the de-facto US standard), Language Mechanics, https://dcmp.org/learn/600-captioning-key---language-mechanics:
  > "Do not emphasize a word using all capital letters except to indicate screaming or shouting."
  And Text section, https://dcmp.org/learn/597:
  > "Mixed case characters are preferred for readability. However, capital letters are used for screaming or shouting."
- **Ofcom** guidelines (archived copy of *Ofcom's Guidelines on Provision of Television Access Services*, https://web.archive.org/web/20210418091807if_/https://www.ofcom.org.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0025/212776/provision-of-tv-access-services-guidelines.pdf), §1.18: capitals are reserved for "louder speech (using capital letters)".
- **The mechanism, spelled out** (Meryl Evans, same post as above):
  > "All caps show zero shape variation. MERYL has a flat top edge and a flat bottom edge. That's it. No change in shape. It's like a rectangle block. … Visual differences in sentence case captions boost readability. The lack of visual differences in UPPERCASE adds friction to the reading experience."

**The dissent** is narrower than short-form editors assume — it is a *compliance* point, not a readability one:
> "There are guidelines that captioning specialists follow, but some are merely customary and do not affect compliance. For example, upper and lower case text is currently the norm, but you'll still be OK if your text is all caps."
— Oliver Peters, "ADA Compliance Considerations in Production and Post", ProVideo Coalition, 2 Dec 2019, https://www.provideocoalition.com/ada-compliance-considerations-in-production-and-post/

And the pro-short-form side treats caps as a deliberate loudness signal: "All caps — High-energy, emphasis content — Increases perceived volume and urgency" (Blitzcut, URL above). **Note that this agrees with the broadcast standards about what caps *mean* — shouting — it just wants that meaning applied to everything.**

### Word-by-word vs phrase chunks — **the standards side treats word-by-word as a live-captioning compromise, not a style**

- BBC §21.5 (Live subtitling): "Live subtitles should appear word by word, from left to right, to allow maximum reading time. Live subtitles are justified left (not centred)." — i.e. word-by-word exists to *cover transcription latency*, and comes bundled with left-justification and two-line scrolling.
- BBC §3.2: "Each subtitle should comprise a single complete sentence."
- BBC §19.1 on cumulative (progressive-reveal) subtitles: "Cumulatives should only be used when there is a good reason to delay part of the subtitle (e.g. dramatic impact/song rhythm) and no other way of doing it".
- Ofcom §1.16: "pre-prepared block subtitles are the best approach to providing accurate, easily legible and well-synchronised subtitles and should be used for pre-recorded programmes."
- Ofcom §1.17: "subtitles should normally comprise a single sentence occupying no more than two lines".
- Meryl Evans, "10 Rules", on the one-word-at-a-time app style:
  > "Oh … my … goodness! I've seen some videos that use an app, which shows the captions … One … word … at … a … time … Picture a two-minute video showing captions one word at a time … FAST. Exhausting! Even for fast readers. You're so focused on following the captions that you ignore the action on the screen. It also makes it harder to understand the message."
  > "Long captions turns them into a reading experience instead of a scanning one."
- Rate limits, if you want a number: Ofcom §1.20 "the speed should not normally exceed 160 to 180 words per minutes for pre-recorded programmes"; BBC §4.1 "a minimum period of around 0.3 seconds per word"; DCMP is stricter still — 130/140/160 wpm by level, https://dcmp.org/learn/601.

**Against:** the whole short-form industry, on eye-tracking/pacing grounds — "keeps the viewer's eye moving, matches the pace of speech" (Blitzcut).

### Centre-screen vs lower-third — **both camps say the same thing: do not cover the face**

- Ofcom §1.15: "Subtitles … should normally occupy the bottom of the screen, except where they would obscure the speaker's mouth or other vital information or activity. **It is particularly important to avoid obscuring the face, as this conveys emotions and tone of voice, as well as being necessary for lip-reading.**"
- DCMP, Caption Placement: "Captions are placed on the bottom two lines, but placement should not interfere with names, faces, or mouths of speakers or text/graphics that are essential to the comprehension of the media." Plus: "(Confusion occurs when captions jump around the screen.)"
- BBC §10.1, and this is the one that matters for a 9:16 deliverable: "In vertical (9:16) videos, it is common to position subtitles a little higher up, though still generally in the lower third of the screen. This is because faces are generally in the top half of the screen, and positioning the subtitles at the bottom makes it harder for the viewer to read the text and see the person speaking."
- BBC also gives hard vertical-format geometry: subtitles must stay inside "the central 75% vertically and the central 90% horizontally" for 9:16; max **3 lines** for vertical (vs 2 for landscape); and "the equivalent to 37 characters in a 75% width region of a 16:9 (landscape) video is **25 characters** in a 90% width region of a 9:16 (vertical) video."
- Meryl Evans: "In multiple informal surveys, 99% of the participants picked the bottom. Stay consistent and keep the captions in one place for the entire video."
- Even the pro-animation side agrees: "Lower-middle third: The standard position for TikTok captions. Avoids the UI buttons on the right side … **The creator's face should remain unobscured.**" (Blitzcut)

**There is no real split here.** Dead-centre-over-the-face captions have no defender in anything I could fetch.

### Outline/stroke vs drop shadow vs solid scrim box — **the standards split by open vs closed**

- DCMP, Font: "The use of white characters is preferred. They need to be **medium weight, be sans serif, have a drop or rim shadow, and be proportionally spaced. The use of a translucent box is preferred** so that the text will be clearer, especially on light backgrounds."
- BBC §8.1: "Most subtitles are typed in white text on a black background to ensure optimum legibility." §8.2: "Background colours are no longer used."
- Ofcom §1.16: "Recommended colours are white, yellow, cyan and green **against a solid black background** as these provide the best contrast."
- 3Play Media splits it cleanly by delivery type: "Closed captions are typically displayed as white text on an opaque or semi-transparent black box. **Subtitles are often styled in white text with a black outline or black drop shadow.**" — "Closed Caption Styling & Formatting Best Practices You Need to Know", 3Play Media, 3 Nov 2023, https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/closed-caption-styling-formatting-best-practices-you-need-to-know/
- Meryl Evans on the transparent-background risk: "The problem with a transparent background is that you can see a lot of movement behind them. They can distract me to the point that I miss the content. If you use a transparent background, use it wisely."
- 3Play again, and this is the governing principle: "**accessibility must be placed before aesthetics**. Using your brand's font and colors may support a consistent brand experience, but they can also be illegible to caption users if a font is too fanciful or colors don't have enough contrast."

Note the **direct collision on yellow**: in BBC (§8.3) and Ofcom (§1.16), yellow is a *speaker-identification* colour — it means "this is a different person speaking." In the short-form template it means "this is the word being spoken right now." Same colour, opposite semantics.

### Emoji in captions — **explicitly banned in the BBC standard**

> §9.3.2: "Emoji versions of characters, for example the musical note symbol, 🎵 (U+1F3B5), **must not be used**, because they can be hard to see against a black background. For the musical note, use ♫ (U+266B) instead."

Against: Submagic markets "Trending styles with emoji" as a feature (per Blitzcut's comparison table). Also worth knowing: BBC restricts broadcast subtitles to a bare ASCII-ish set — "A-Z a-z 0-9 ! ) ( , . ? : -" plus a short list.

### One more, unprompted but relevant: animation itself

Meryl Evans's rule for roll-up captions ends with: "**Do not fade in or fade out.** … Why no fading? Because the animation from fading adds friction to the caption experience."

# 4. Is there a different standard for serious / news / courtroom content?

**Yes — and for your specific case it is sharper than a taste question. It is an authenticity problem.**

### The written standards also carve out serious content

- **Ofcom §1.20** flags "commissioning editors and producers should be aware that dialogue which would require subtitles faster than 200 wpm would be difficult for many viewers to follow."
- **BBC §2.11** — directly relevant to courtroom profanity: "Do not edit out strong language unless it is absolutely impossible to edit elsewhere in the sentence - deaf or hard-of-hearing viewers find this extremely irritating and condescending."
- **BBC §4.2.5** Critical information: "In a news item or factual content, the main aim is to convey the 'what, when, who, how, why'."
- **DCMP Presentation Rate** carves out an explicit verbatim rule that covers your footage exactly: "The only times when presentation rate is ignored are **when any person is quoted**, a well-known or famous person is speaking on-screen or off-screen, poems and other published works are quoted, and/or song lyrics are sung. **These must be captioned verbatim.**" — i.e. a judge's or defendant's words are in the never-paraphrase category.
- **BBC §5.5**: "Do not bring in any dramatic subtitles too early." — no pre-empting the beat.

# 5. What reads as credible vs clickbait, concretely

**Credible (each backed above):**
- Sentence case, one colour, white, medium-weight sans serif — DCMP/BBC/Ofcom.
- Phrase or sentence chunks that hold long enough to read; 160–180 wpm ceiling; ≥0.3s per word.
- Lower third, held in **one fixed position** for the whole video; moved only to avoid covering a face, a mouth, or an on-screen name/court ID. Oliver Peters' specific case: "if you have a lower-third name graphic come on screen, you want to move any caption from its usual lower-third, safe-title position to the top of the screen while that name graphic is visible."
- Translucent black scrim or a black drop/rim shadow — not a bright brand colour.
- No fades, no bounce, no scale-punch, no per-word colour switching.
- Verbatim, including profanity and stumbles where they carry meaning: Meryl Evans on stumbles — "in TV shows, if a character is stumbling, they're obviously nervous, or it tells you something about their personality and how they're responding to a situation, it can communicate a lot of things. We don't have sound cues, so we may not hear that nervousness in their voice." (Jeff Frick, "Accurate vs. Easy to Read: The Captioning Tradeoff", 7 Aug 2026, https://turnthelens.substack.com/p/accurate-vs-easy-to-read-the-captioning)
- Speaker identification done the boring way — a bracketed `[Judge]` / `[Defendant]`, or a dash. Meryl Evans: "Some captioners use uppercase letters for speaker identification like this: MERYL: Please use sentence case captions. Here's a better way to improve readability: [Meryl] Please use sentence case captions."
- The governing summary: "The key to great captions is invisibility. … If I notice something about the captions, that's usually not a good sign."

**Clickbait / slop-adjacent:**
- Per-word yellow/gold highlight rolling window (reads as the CapCut/Submagic/Captions.ai default preset; and in broadcast grammar, yellow means "different speaker").
- ALL CAPS body text (in standards grammar, caps mean *shouting* — applied to a judge's whole ruling it editorialises).
- Captions parked centre-frame over faces.
- Bounce/pop/scale animation and fades.
- Emoji.
- Any AI voiceover anywhere near it — that is the tell working court journalists actually name.
- The #Courtroom* hashtag cluster from the Lead Stories prebunk.
- Cheesy dramatic score.

## Direct flags for the 55-second Texas courtroom short

1. **The biggest risk is not "looking hustle-bro" — it's being taken for AI.** There is a large, actively fact-checked genre of fabricated vertical courtroom shorts as of Feb–Aug 2026. Anything that matches its surface — dramatic on-screen text, a synthetic narrator, the #Courtroom* hashtags, a channel of near-identical uploads — pushes real footage into the fake bucket. Consider the inverse move: on-screen sourcing (court, case number, date) as a persistent element, which the fake genre never does.
2. **9:16 geometry is specified, not guessed**: BBC gives you ~25 characters per line max, up to 3 lines, inside the central 75% vertical / 90% horizontal, sitting in the lower third but nudged up so it clears the speaker's face.
3. **Never cover the face.** Ofcom's wording — "particularly important to avoid obscuring the face, as this conveys emotions and tone of voice" — is exactly the thing a judge/defendant exchange is *made of*. Centre-frame captions destroy the only content you have.
4. **Verbatim, including profanity.** BBC §2.11 and DCMP's quoted-speech carve-out both land on this. Cleaning up a defendant's words is both a standards violation and a credibility leak.
5. **A defensible, boring house style is itself the differentiator** here — "Great captions are boring!" is the whole argument, and in a feed of fake courtroom drama it is also the authenticity signal.

## What our current build violates, measured against the above

Checked against `scripts/caption_thompson_style.py` and the rendered
`spidermonkeyshort_FINAL.mp4`:

| Our setting | Standard says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| ALL CAPS throughout | caps = shouting only (BBC §11.2, DCMP) | violates |
| gold `&H0000D7FF` on live word | yellow = *different speaker* (BBC §8.3, Ofcom §1.16); one colour only (Evans) | violates, and inverts the meaning |
